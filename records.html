Public records · The Shoals

Between the City of Florence and Lauderdale County, they have spent ninety thousand dollars watching you.

Not a rumor. Not our opinion. These are signed contracts and approved resolutions, pulled from the government's own websites. Here's what was signed, when, by whom, and for how much. We'll add every other city as we get their records.

Signed contract

Lauderdale County — $30,000

On April 8, 2024, the Lauderdale County Commission voted unanimously to sign an agreement with Flock Group, Inc. (Flock Safety) for its automated license plate reader system. The county agreed to pay ten thousand dollars a year, for three years — thirty thousand dollars total — out of the General Fund.

The resolution was signed by all five commissioners: Chairman Danny Pettus, Roger Garner, Brad Black, Fay Parker, and Joe Hackworth. The contract covers four Flock "Falcon" cameras and the Flock software platform. The motion to approve was made by Commissioner Brad Black and seconded by Commissioner Fay Parker.

The details, from the contract itself Three-year initial term that automatically renews for two more years unless the county cancels it. Thirty-day data retention. And critically, the system is connected to a statewide and a nationwide lookup network — meaning agencies far outside the Shoals can search plates captured by these cameras, and vice versa.

Approved by the City Council

City of Florence — $60,000

On December 5, 2023, the Florence City Council approved a contract with Flock Group, Inc. for the Florence Police Department. The city agreed to pay twenty thousand dollars a year, for three years — sixty thousand dollars total — from budgeted funds, out of Account #256-42100.

The resolution was sponsored by Ron Tyler, who was the Florence Police Chief at the time and is now the Mayor of Florence.

What this means

Why ninety thousand dollars should bother you

This is public money — your money — spent on a system that records where residents drive, without a warrant and without most people ever being asked. Ninety thousand dollars is the figure for just two of our local governments. It does not yet include Muscle Shoals, Sheffield, or Tuscumbia.

The part most people don't realize is where the data goes. These cameras aren't a closed local system. Per the county's own contract, they're tied into a statewide and nationwide license plate lookup network. That means a plate captured by a camera here in the Shoals can be searched by law enforcement agencies in other counties and other states — agencies with no connection to our community and no accountability to the people who paid for the cameras. Your daily movements, logged here, become searchable far from here.

That's the trade our local governments signed us up for: our tax dollars, our movements, fed into a national surveillance network, on contracts most residents never heard about until now.

Wait — how do you even get these documents? What a public records request (FOIA) actually is

You might have heard the term "FOIA" (Freedom of Information Act). Here's the plain version. Government records — contracts, meeting minutes, budgets, policies — belong to the public. You paid for them. So the law gives ordinary people the right to ask for copies.

At the federal level it's called FOIA. Here in Alabama, the state version is the Alabama Open Records Act, and it's what covers our local police departments, city councils, and the county. Under it, any Alabama resident can send a written request to a government office asking for specific records, and the office is supposed to provide them within a set timeframe.

It's not always fast, and Alabama's law has weak teeth — agencies sometimes drag their feet, and the main way to force the issue is a lawsuit. But it's how sunlight gets in. Every contract on this page exists because someone asked for the public's own records. It's one of the most powerful, least-used tools a regular citizen has.
